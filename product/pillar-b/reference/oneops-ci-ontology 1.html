§ 01 · Start here

What this document is, and how to read it.

The OneOps CI Ontology is the model of what an enterprise runs — the catalogue of things the platform can represent (a pod, a database, an availability zone, a business capability) and the rules for how they connect. It is the second of the two pillars of the OneOps Service-Centric Knowledge Graph; the first, the relationship layer, is documented separately. This ontology was designed over a twelve-task program that began at "what, precisely, is a configuration item" and ended at a frozen v0.1 contract that engineering can build against: thirty-seven concrete classes and six abstract parents, eleven stored relationship types, a write-time allowlist that rejects malformed relationships at the API boundary, an architecture specified to fourteen components, and a product specified to fifteen capabilities. This document is the single reference that survives the program — the version the twelve working files were written to produce.

It is built to be read by three audiences at three depths. An executive can read the strategic sections and leave with the full argument — the problem OneOps attacks, the four claims it can demonstrate in a customer call, and the honest case a competitor would make against it. An engineer can read the design and architecture sections and find the precision needed to build. And the section on the four white-space differentiators sits in the middle deliberately: strategic enough for a CXO, specific enough for an architect. For the non-technical reader

A configuration item, or CI, is a single thing an IT team runs — a server, an application, a database. A CI ontology is the agreed list of kinds of things, plus the rules for how they relate. Most enterprises run one inside a system called a CMDB, and most of those models have grown sprawling and untrustworthy. This document describes a deliberately small, deliberately opinionated replacement — small enough that a human can hold it in their head, and structured enough that an AI can reason over it.

What follows is a narrative, not a task-by-task digest. The twelve working files are the record of how the decisions were made; this document is the decisions themselves, organized around what a reader needs to know rather than the order in which the work happened. Where a claim rests on a specific piece of working-record reasoning, the relevant task is cited inline (like this · Task 04) so the reader who wants the full argument knows which file to open. The version is v0.1, and it is frozen: the analysis phase is over, and the next thing this program produces should be a running system.

The strategic case

The most expensive system of record in the enterprise is wrong in most enterprises.

And the people who depend on it have quietly stopped believing it. This is the failure OneOps is built to fix — not with better discipline, but with a different architecture.

§ 02 · The problem and the bet

A four-to-five-billion-dollar market for a product that mostly does not work.

The CMDB is the system of record an enterprise's entire service-management apparatus is supposed to rest on — the authoritative map of what infrastructure exists, what depends on what, and what breaks when something fails. ServiceNow, BMC, and OpenText UCMDB sell that system of record to most of the Fortune 500, in a market worth four to five billion dollars a year. The uncomfortable fact the industry has learned to talk around is that the product mostly does not work.

The defensible version of the statistic, stated carefully because the program took care to get it right, is Gartner's 2019 finding that only about a quarter of CMDB initiatives achieve meaningful value (Standards_Heritage). The more famous claim — that eighty percent of CMDBs fail — circulates without an accessible primary source, and OneOps does not rely on it. The conservative number is damning enough. Stated the other way: in roughly three of four enterprises, the service map the CIO believes is driving incident response, change management, and capacity planning is materially wrong, and no one can easily tell which three-quarters they are in.

This is not a data-entry problem that better discipline would fix. It is two structural failures, and the research traces both precisely. The first is a velocity mismatch. Classic CMDBs were built on a weekly or nightly reconciliation cadence, which was adequate when a server lived for three years and is absurd when a Kubernetes pod lives for ninety seconds. A system of record that reconciles weekly against an estate that changes by the second is wrong the moment it is written. The second is the absence of provenance. Incumbent CMDBs present every fact as flat truth — with no record of which source asserted it, at what confidence, or how stale it is. So when the map is wrong, no one can distinguish a bad probe from a stale manual entry from a reconciliation error, and the only rational response is to stop trusting the map entirely. Engineering teams across the enterprise market have already made that choice. The CMDB is funded as a system of record and used as a historical curiosity.

The executive framing is therefore not "CMDBs have data-quality issues." It is sharper and more useful than that: the most expensive system of record in the IT estate is wrong in most enterprises, the people who depend on it have quietly stopped believing it, and the incumbents cannot fix it without rebuilding the architectural foundations that made them incumbents in the first place.

Why now

Three changes — not market moods, but changes in what is architecturally possible or necessary — make this the moment a different design can win (Task 12 · Part One B).

The first is that the velocity mismatch has become irreparable rather than merely uncomfortable. As long as estates were mostly long-lived virtual machines, the weekly-sync model degraded gracefully: the map was stale but not nonsense. Cloud-native architecture broke that gracefully-degrading property. An estate whose unit of compute is an ephemeral pod managed by a declarative controller cannot be modeled by a system that assumes durable hosts and periodic reconciliation — and the incumbents' own responses are the evidence. BMC declines to synchronize pods at all; UCMDB bolts Kubernetes objects onto a nine-level host tree designed for physical servers. They are not failing to adopt cloud-native modeling out of negligence. Their architectures cannot absorb it, and that is a durable opening.

The second is that graph-native storage has matured past the point where it was a research bet. ServiceNow's table-per-class relational architecture — hundreds of tables, a JOIN cliff at scale — is precisely what makes its CMDB both expensive to operate and structurally resistant to the small, fast schema changes cloud-native modeling demands. A decade ago, replacing it with a graph database meant betting on immature technology. Today, property-graph databases with the traversal performance and operational tooling to serve a production CMDB exist, and the architectural penalty that once justified the relational design is gone. The substrate that sinks ServiceNow at scale is now optional.

The third is that AI grounding requires a structured knowledge graph, which turns an aspiration into a competitive necessity. The market is saturated with "AI-native" claims, most of them indistinguishable from marketing. But the substantive version of the claim is architectural: an AI agent reasoning about an enterprise's services — its blast radius, its dependencies, its ownership — needs a structured, relationship-rich, provenance-bearing graph to reason over, not a relational schema it must flatten and guess at. Enterprises that want AI to help operate their estate are discovering that the prerequisite is a knowledge graph they do not have. That makes a service-centric knowledge graph not a feature but the foundation AI-native ITSM has to stand on — and the incumbents' relational foundations are the wrong shape for it.

The thesis

The architectural thesis is captured in one line from the competitive research: flat and legible like an internal developer platform on top, deep and reconciled like a CMDB underneath (Competitive_Reference_Analysis). The internal developer platforms — Backstage, Cortex — got legibility right and depth wrong; they are easy to read and shallow on reconciliation and dependency. The CMDBs got depth right and legibility wrong; UCMDB in particular has the reconciliation engine and the compute-truths-at-read-time pattern OneOps deliberately inherits, and it still failed in market because its query language was too hard for ordinary users to read the graph it so carefully built. OneOps's bet is that neither half is optional.

Underneath the line sit the architectural commitments the design program made concrete: store facts and compute truths, so that blast radius and critical path are derived at read time and never stored as fact; carry evidence on every artifact, so that every CI and edge knows its source, confidence, and freshness; name relationships in the direction English reads, and keep the vocabulary deliberately small — eleven edges and thirty-seven classes against ServiceNow's fifty-three relationship types and four-hundred-plus tables; and enforce correctness at write time, so the triplet allowlist rejects malformed relationships at the API boundary rather than letting the graph rot. These are not features to be marketed. They are the structural answers to the two failures above.

The strategic thesis is a claim about an underserved market. ServiceNow is too expensive and too complex for the mid-market; its breadth becomes a liability when a fifty-person engineering organization has to operate it. BMC and UCMDB are too foreign to contemporary engineering teams; their heritage and their schema-as-code-on-a-CRM-platform workflows belong to a different era of IT. The observability vendors and the developer platforms are too shallow on the CMDB side; they see traffic and ownership but not reconciled, evidence-bearing dependency. There is a gap in the middle — the mid-market enterprise that has outgrown spreadsheets and developer portals but cannot justify ServiceNow, that has adopted Kubernetes and needs a system of record that can model it, and that wants AI grounding without buying a phantom. The incumbents are too heavy for that gap; the disruptors are too light. OneOps is built for it.

The thesis in one line
ServiceNow built a CMDB to document. BMC built one to prove. UCMDB built one to query. OneOps is building one for the world the others were not built for — where a trustworthy small graph beats an untrustworthy large one, and where AI has to reason over the map in real time.

What OneOps gives up

The CMDBs that win take strong positions, and a strong position excludes customers. Stating the exclusions is what makes the rest of this argument credible to a reader who has heard every vendor claim to be for everyone. OneOps v1.0 gives up four things, each named precisely in the working record. It gives up deep audit and federated identity: the security module and the federated-identity integration a regulated enterprise requires are not in v1.0 (Task 11 · CR4). It gives up probe breadth comparable to ServiceNow: v1.0 ships a selective set of discovery probes that meet the evidence-confidence floor, not the dozens ServiceNow ships (Task 11 · CR2). It gives up cross-class lineage: a workload re-architected from pods to serverless cannot be traced as a single lineage in v0.1 (Task 08 · D4). And it gives up first-class API and protection modeling: API provision is modeled with a disclosed semantic strain, and protection is a holding action, both deferred to v0.3 (Task 07 · G1/G2). A reader should take this list as confidence rather than apology. A vendor that cannot name what it gives up has not made the choices that produce a coherent product.

The bet

Stated plainly: the mid-market enterprise — five hundred to five thousand employees, actively adopting Kubernetes, often either migrating off a decayed ServiceNow CMDB or starting greenfield — will pay more for legibility, evidence, and genuine AI grounding than it will pay for ServiceNow's breadth or BMC's compliance depth. The wager is that in this segment a trustworthy small graph beats an untrustworthy large one, and that the segment is large enough and underserved enough to build a company on. What would prove the bet right in twelve to eighteen months is customer acquisition concentrated in that segment, demo-to-close conversion driven by the four demonstrable claims rather than feature checklists, and low first-year churn because the graph stayed legible and the evidence model held trust. What would prove it wrong is named with equal precision in §9 — because a bet stated so that the board can ask "where do we stand against these signals" is worth more than one that can only be reported as going well.

§ 03 · The four demonstrable claims

Four things a customer can watch, not four things a deck asserts.

OneOps's strategic claims are credible only because they can be shown in a customer call. Four demos carry every executive conversation for the next twelve months, and each is the visible surface of a white-space differentiator validated in cross-platform testing and built as a shipping product capability. They are the substance behind the positioning; the section that follows explains the design beneath them.

D1 · Namespace-inherited ownership · CAP8 / W1
Reassign one namespace; watch three hundred pods' ownership follow.

Change the owner of a Kubernetes namespace, and every pod, config, and secret it contains reflects the new owner instantly — with zero per-pod writes. At Kubernetes churn rates, ServiceNow re-stamps an "owner" field tens of thousands of times for an owner that never changes, and goes stale between syncs anyway. OneOps puts the owner on the stable thing and lets the churning things inherit.

Who finds it decisive

Platform engineers and SREs running real Kubernetes estates — anyone who has watched a CMDB rot against pod churn.

D2 · Evidence distinct from claim · CAP10 / W2
Observed traffic stays evidence until you promote it.

Show observed communicatesWith traffic, then promote a real dependency to dependsOn in one action — and the dependency map contains curated dependencies, not packet captures. ServiceNow's dependency maps are noisy because every observed connection becomes a "Depends on": health checks, telemetry scrapes, and real dependencies indistinguishable. OneOps keeps observation and claim separate, so the map is defensible in a root-cause analysis.

Who finds it decisive

Service owners who curate dependencies; SREs who learned to ignore the incumbent dependency map because it is noise.

D3 · Evidence on every artifact · CAP9 / W3
Ask why the graph believes this; get a traceable answer.

Click any CI or edge and see its sources, confidence scores, and freshness. Ask why A depends on B and get a real answer — "a human declared it on date X," or "promoted from forty traffic observations on date Y." Incumbent CMDBs present facts as flat truth with no provenance; when the map is wrong, no one can tell why, so no one trusts it. OneOps makes every fact answer for itself.

Who finds it decisive

Compliance officers, who need audit lineage, and AI skeptics, who need to verify a suggestion's grounding — two audiences, one demo.

D4 · The de-overloaded blast radius · CAP6 / dependsOn
Blast radius with no hosting or traffic noise.

From a failing CI, get a clean, deterministic impact list — directly-impacted and potentially-impacted — with protection and management edges correctly excluded. ServiceNow's blast radius inherits the overloaded "Depends on," so the list is polluted by hosting and traffic edges and responders distrust it. OneOps splits what ServiceNow conflates, which is what makes the impact list trustworthy under live-incident pressure.

Who finds it decisive

SREs and incident commanders under live-incident pressure, where a noisy blast radius is worse than none at all.

The floor beneath the four · CAP5 · graph legibility

The four demos all assume one thing: that the graph is legible — that a human can see and navigate it. This is not a fifth differentiator; it is the foundation the four stand on. UCMDB is the cautionary tale that makes the point unavoidable. It had the reconciliation engine and the compute-truths-at-read-time pattern OneOps is rebuilding, and it failed in market anyway because its query language made the graph unreadable to ordinary users. A better backend that no one can read is worth nothing.

If service mapping and dependency visualization ship and the graph is illegible, OneOps repeats UCMDB's exact failure with a better engine — which is why the product work named it the single make-or-break capability (Task 11 · Part Four §4). The four demos are the differentiation. Legibility is the price of admission.

§ 04 · The white-space differentiators · read by everyone

The four things no incumbent does — each with its honest cost.

This is the intellectual heart of the document, and the section both audiences should read. Each of the four differentiators below is something no incumbent CMDB does; each is sharp enough for an engineer to build against and clear enough for an executive to sell. And each carries a real cost — because a white-space section that is all upside is brochure copy, not validation. The structure is the same for all four: what it is, why no incumbent does it, what it gives customers, what it costs, and the honest answer to the strongest objection a competitor would raise.

W1 · Namespace-inherited ownership
Put the owner on the stable thing; let the churning things inherit.
2-hop
Owner query, not a field read
What it is

Ownership of namespaced Kubernetes resources — pods, workload controllers, config maps, secrets, in-cluster data stores — resolves through the chain contains ← Namespace plus Namespace ownedBy Party. Everything else carries a direct ownedBy edge (Task 06 · §A.1).

Why no incumbent does it

ServiceNow carries "Owned by" as a flat attribute on every record — every pod, every object, re-stamped with an owner. UCMDB ties owner through a per-CI party relationship. BMC implies it through the hosting hierarchy. All three write ownership per CI, which means all three rewrite it on every churn.

What it gives customers

One ownership change governs hundreds of contained CIs. Reassign a namespace to a new team and every pod, config, and secret it contains follows, with zero per-object writes. At Kubernetes churn rates — pods living minutes — this is the difference between an ownership model that stays current and one that rots. It is the velocity-mismatch argument turned into a design: the namespace is the stable anchor, and the fast-moving things inherit from it rather than each carrying a copy that goes stale.

The honest cost

"Who owns this pod" becomes a two-hop traversal rather than a single attribute read. For ad-hoc queries and naive integrations expecting a flat owner field, that is genuinely more work, and a customer migrating dashboards that read owner off the CI directly will have to rewrite them. The architecture answers the ergonomics with a read-time resolver that returns the inherited owner in one call (Task 08 · D7) — but the data contract is two hops, and that is real.

The honest counterargument

"But ServiceNow just has an owned-by field on every CI." Correct — and at fifty thousand pods churning hourly, ServiceNow rewrites that field fifty thousand times for an owner that never changes, and goes stale between syncs. OneOps made the owner live on the stable thing and the churning things inherit; the trade accepted is that the pod-ownership query is two hops, not one. For an estate that is mostly static VMs, this model gives nothing extra — those carry a direct owner, exactly like the incumbents'.

W2 · communicatesWith as evidence, not claim
A dependency is something someone stands behind, not a packet capture.
+1
Edge that incumbents fold away
What it is

Observed traffic is a separate edge — communicatesWith, carrying an observation_frequency attribute — that never auto-promotes to dependsOn. Promotion to a dependency claim is an explicit decision: a human confirmation or a service-mapping session, never an automatic upgrade (Task 06 · §G.2).

Why no incumbent does it

ServiceNow, BMC, and UCMDB fold observed traffic into "Depends on." They cannot distinguish "we saw these two services exchange packets" from "this service requires that service to function." UCMDB's talk relationship is the nearest analogue and still carries weaker, less-auditable semantics. The true conceptual peer — OpenTelemetry's service graph — lives in observability, not the CMDB.

What it gives customers

A clean dependency-promotion workflow, where evidence accumulates and a human or a rule promotes it; honest confidence scoring, where a dependsOn is a claim someone stands behind rather than a side effect of a health check; and auditable dependency provenance, where you can ask why the graph believes A depends on B and get either "someone declared it" or "promoted from forty observations." This is the difference between a dependency map that survives a root-cause analysis and one that responders learned to ignore.

The honest cost

One extra edge type in the model, and an explicit promotion step that automation must implement — observed traffic does not become dependency for free. A team that wants "saw traffic, assume dependency" (the ServiceNow default) has to build the promotion logic OneOps deliberately withholds. For a low-maturity shop, the incumbent's auto-fold is less work upfront. OneOps is betting that the cost of the promotion step is smaller than the cost of a dependency graph nobody trusts.

The honest counterargument

"But ServiceNow auto-discovers dependencies from traffic." It auto-discovers traffic and calls it dependency — which is exactly why ServiceNow dependency maps are famously noisy with health-check and telemetry edges nobody curated. OneOps keeps the observation and makes a human or a rule promote it. The cost is the promotion step; the benefit is a dependency graph you can defend in an incident review.

W3 · succeedsLifecycleOf as first-class genealogy
One edge, five subtypes, a queryable lineage — not a stashed string.
5
Genealogy subtypes, one edge
What it is

A first-class lineage edge with a subtype enum — version, DR, backup, migration, rollback — and a per-class identity-scope rule (Task 06 · §K). One edge expresses the full genealogy of a thing across its successive forms.

Why no incumbent does it

ServiceNow splits the concept across separate relationship types — "Upgrade of," "DR provided by," "Migrated from." BMC and UCMDB overwrite records or stash predecessors in ad-hoc previous_version fields. None has a unified, queryable genealogy edge.

What it gives customers

DR-pair tracking (which instance fails over to which), version-succession queries (show this service's lineage across six deploys), and backup-restore lineage — all first-class, queryable, and subtyped. For audit, for a root-cause analysis on "the version we deployed Tuesday," and for change-risk on successive versions, this is structurally better than a flat previous_version string that the next deploy overwrites.

The honest cost

Succession is a declared or pipeline-driven fact, not a discovered one. A deploy pipeline must emit the version edge; a DR config must declare the pair. Nothing populates succeedsLifecycleOf from a probe. If a customer's pipelines do not emit it, the edge is empty and the genealogy benefit is theoretical. ServiceNow's "Upgrade of" carries the same write burden, so this is no worse than the incumbents — but it is not free, and a customer expecting discovered genealogy will be disappointed.

The honest counterargument

"But UCMDB tracks versions automatically." UCMDB tracks the current version attribute and overwrites history; it does not retain a queryable predecessor chain unless you build one. OneOps gives the chain as a first-class edge — but you have to emit it from your pipeline. If you have no deploy automation, neither model helps.

W4 · The managed flag replaces phantom control-plane CIs
Your graph holds the things you run, not a shadow of AWS's control plane.
0
Phantom cloud-account CIs
What it is

OneOps does not model "AWS account manages RDS" as an edge. The fact that a resource is provider-orchestrated is carried by a managed=true attribute on the individual class (Task 06 · §H.2) — not by a synthetic manager node.

Why no incumbent does it

ServiceNow and UCMDB invent phantom cloud-account and control-plane CIs, plus manages-style relationships, to make cloud orchestration modelable. The result is a proliferation of CIs that represent the provider's control plane rather than the customer's estate — nodes the customer never asked for and then has to maintain.

What it gives customers

No proliferation of cloud-orchestration CIs. The graph contains the customer's actual resources, not a synthetic shadow of the cloud provider's control plane, and counts stay honest: a five-thousand-CI estate is five thousand things you run, not eight thousand including phantom accounts and control planes. Honest counts matter when the whole product is a system of record you are trying to make trustworthy.

The honest cost

Cross-account questions — "show everything in the prod AWS account" — become attribute filters (WHERE provider=aws AND account=prod) rather than graph traversals from an account node. For customers whose mental model is "the cloud account is a node I navigate from," this is a genuine reframing, and any tooling built to traverse from an account CI has nothing to traverse from. The information is all present; the access pattern is filter, not walk.

The honest counterargument

"But ServiceNow shows me the cloud account as a CI I can drill into." It shows you a CI that represents Amazon's billing boundary, not anything you operate — and then you maintain it. OneOps makes account an attribute you filter on. If your governance model is account-centric, "navigate from the account" becoming "filter by the account" is real work; if it is service-centric — which the whole knowledge graph assumes — you never wanted the phantom node.

A note on narrowness

Each differentiator is sharp for a specific slice and dull elsewhere — namespace inheritance matters at Kubernetes scale, the promotion workflow matters where dependency curation is a real practice, genealogy matters for audit-heavy customers. That is the segmentation strategy stated as a feature, not a weakness: a differentiator that mattered equally to everyone would be a differentiator for no one. The concession inside the concession is that OneOps's growth is therefore gated on those segments being large enough, which §9 names honestly.

§ 05 · The design — classes, edges, and the v0.1 contract

Thirty-seven concrete classes, eleven edges, and a write-time allowlist.

The ontology is deliberately small. Where ServiceNow ships several hundred CI classes and BMC inherits the deep DMTF CIM hierarchy, OneOps ships thirty-seven concrete classes and six abstract parents, organized into five abstraction-layered domains. The discipline is stated as a rule in Task 04: (Task 04 · 5d) default to an attribute, not a new class — a thing earns class status only when an edge needs to point at it or a query needs to filter on it. Every class below survived that test; the register in Appendix A records the ones that did not. The full per-class metadata — attributes, identity rules, discovery sources — lives in the working record (Task 05b); what follows is the reference catalogue.

The five domains

The domains are abstraction layers, not deployment tiers. Business is what the enterprise does and funds; Application is the logical software that delivers it; Platform is the orchestration substrate applications run on; Infrastructure is the compute, network, and storage beneath the platform; Facility is the physical and cloud-topological ground everything sits on. The ordering matters because the realizes spine climbs it: a pod realizes an application service, which realizes a business application. Reading a service top-to-bottom is reading down the layers; computing a blast radius is reading back up them.

The eleven stored edges

Relationships are typed and closed. There are eleven stored edge types — facts written and persisted — and four computed edge types that are never stored but resolved at read time from the eleven. The direction convention is fixed and matches English (Task 04 · P04): the edge reads subject-verb-object left to right. Every stored edge carries an impact_bearing flag, and this is the single most consequential mechanism in the model: it defaults to true, because a flag that defaulted false would silently drop edges out of every blast-radius computation and no one would notice until an incident.

The part has no independent existence; deleting the whole cascades to the parts. Identity-contributing — the part is identified partly by its whole.

A boundary or grouping. Unlike composedOf, the contained survives the container's deletion. Namespace contains Pod; Cluster contains Namespace.
runsOn entity → host · N:1
Execution dependency on a host. Identity-contributing. The absence of a runsOn edge is meaningful — it signals a provider-managed resource (see the managed flag).
dependsOn dependent → provider · N:M
A declared functional dependency. The deliberate catch-all — which is exactly why six anti-patterns (Appendix) keep it from absorbing relationships that belong to more specific edges.
communicatesWith peer → peer · N:M · asymmetric
Observed network communication. Evidence, not a claim — it records that traffic was seen, and is promoted to dependsOn only by a deliberate human or policy step. The heart of differentiator W2.
realizes realizer → concept · N:M
Implements a higher-abstraction concept. The vertical spine: the chain criticalPath walks from business concept to infrastructure. Carries a second flag, spine_bearing.
memberOf member → collection · N:M
Membership in a set or group with no compositional or hosting semantics — the weakest grouping edge, used where nothing stronger is true.
manages manager → managed · N:M
Not impact-bearing. Control-plane management — a WorkloadController managing its Pods. Deliberately excluded from blast radius: managing a thing is not depending on it, and conflating the two is a known incumbent error.
ownedBy CI → Party · N:1
Accountable ownership, pointing at a Party. Frequently not set per-CI but inherited from the CI's Namespace at read time — differentiator W1.
locatedIn entity → place · N:1
Physical or topological location, pointing into the Facility domain. The basis on which sharesFailureDomain is computed.
succeedsLifecycleOf successor → predecessor · N:1
First-class lifecycle genealogy with a subtype enum (version / dr / backup / migration / rollback). Declared, not discovered — differentiator W3.
Computed — resolved at read time, never stored
impactedBy Directly / Potentially
Blast radius. Walks runsOn, dependsOn, composedOf, and realizes backward; skips any edge where impact_bearing=false; never traverses manages or communicatesWith. Split into Directly- and Potentially-impacted — differentiator behind claim D4.
criticalPath business → infrastructure
The realization chain from a business concept down to the infrastructure carrying it. Traverses the realizes spine, skipping edges where spine_bearing=false.
sharesFailureDomain co-location risk
Co-location risk computed via locatedIn into Facility. Three states, including an explicit failure_domain_unknown sentinel — the system says "I don't know" rather than implying safety it cannot prove.
reaches security reachability · v0.2
Network reachability for attack-path analysis, via communicatesWith plus dependsOn into IdentityProvider. Deferred to v0.2; the class model (IdentityProvider) is already in place to support it.
The bearing-flag mechanism

Two flags govern which edges the computed traversals follow. impact_bearing (on every edge, default true) controls inclusion in impactedBy; spine_bearing (on realizes, default true) controls inclusion in criticalPath. The default direction is the entire point. An edge that should not carry impact is the exception an engineer must consciously mark; the safe behavior — counting an edge as load-bearing until told otherwise — is what you get for free. Register item D6 adopts this for v0.1; risk R1 names enforcing the default as the single highest-priority correctness guarantee in the build.

§ 06 · The seven house design principles

The commitments every decision was checked against.

Seven principles were locked in Task 04 and governed every subsequent call. They are not aspirations; they are the test a proposed class, edge, or attribute had to pass. Each commits OneOps to something specific and, more usefully, rules something out — a principle that forbids nothing decides nothing. Where a later decision was hard, it was hard because two principles pulled against each other, and the register records which won.

01
Task 04 · P01
Keep the vocabulary small

Commits to a closed, hand-curated set of classes and edges small enough to hold in your head. Rules out the incumbent reflex of minting a class per discovered thing — the path that produced ServiceNow's several hundred classes and the class explosion the whole program is a reaction against. The operational form is rule 5d: default to an attribute; a new class must justify itself against an edge or a query.

02
Task 04 · P02
Separate ground truth from derived truth

Commits to a hard line between what is written (the eleven stored edges) and what is computed (the four read-time edges). Rules out storing a blast radius, a critical path, or a failure domain as a fact — because a stored derivation goes stale silently and becomes a lie the system tells with confidence. Everything derived is recomputed from ground truth on every read.

03
Task 04 · P03
Evidence on every artifact

Commits every CI and every edge to carrying its provenance — source, confidence, freshness, and TTL. Rules out the unattributed assertion: the CMDB that shows a dependency with no record of how it knows, which is how incumbent graphs decay into things operators stop trusting. This principle is the substance behind claim D3 and differentiator W2's distinction between observed and declared.

04
Task 04 · P04
Direction and naming match the mental model

Commits edges to reading subject-verb-object in plain English and pointing the way a person would expect (part composedOf whole is wrong; whole composedOf part is right). Rules out direction conventions that are internally consistent but require a lookup table to read — the small daily friction that makes a query language feel hostile and an operator distrust their own traversals.

05
Task 04 · P05
Schema enforces correctness at write time

Commits to rejecting malformed relationships at the API boundary via the triplet allowlist, not flagging them in a later reconciliation pass. Rules out the repair-on-read model where bad data is admitted and cleaned up afterward — the model that guarantees the graph is always somewhat wrong. If a write violates the allowlist, it fails; the graph is correct by construction.

06
Task 04 · P06
Edge types and class definitions are config, not schema

Commits the class and edge vocabularies to living as runtime configuration a customer can extend without a schema migration or a release. Rules out hard-coding the ontology into the storage layer — the rigidity that makes incumbent CMDBs a professional-services engagement to change. The cost, paid honestly, is that validation happens at runtime against config rather than being enforced by the database itself.

07
Task 04 · P07
Ship a prescriptive default out of the box

Commits to shipping the thirty-seven classes, eleven edges, and full allowlist as an opinionated working default — not an empty framework the customer must populate. Rules out the blank-canvas CMDB that technically can model anything and therefore models nothing until a six-month consulting engagement fills it. Extensibility (P06) is the escape hatch; the prescriptive default is the product.

The architecture is specified to fourteen components and seven named risks. One component — the graph store everything else sits on — is deliberately left undecided, because deciding it without the benchmark would be the kind of premature commitment the rest of the design exists to avoid.

CMP4 — the graph store is a known unknown

Every other component assumes a graph store; which one is not yet decided, and pretending otherwise would violate the design's own honesty. The default lean is Neo4j, but the decision is gated on benchmarking the three validation scenarios — the vCenter stack, the Kubernetes multi-pod application, and the multi-tier banking application — against four candidates: Neo4j, Memgraph, ArangoDB, and Postgres + Apache AGE. The candidates differ on exactly the axes the model stresses: read-time traversal latency for the computed edges, write throughput under discovery burst, and the single-node ceiling that risk R2 names for Neo4j's Community edition. The benchmark decides; the architecture is built to keep the store swappable until it does.

The two mechanisms

The bearing-flag enforcement. Detailed in §5: every edge carries impact_bearing defaulting true, every realizes edge carries spine_bearing defaulting true, and the computed traversals honor them. Architecturally this lives in CMP6 (defaults applied on write) and CMP8 (defaults honored on read). It is named again here because R1 makes enforcing the default the highest-priority correctness guarantee in the system — a regression that flipped the default to false would not error; it would quietly return blast radiuses that are too small, and the failure would surface as a missed dependency during an incident.

The namespace-ownership resolver. The mechanism behind differentiator W1. Rather than store an ownedBy edge on every CI, OneOps lets a CI inherit ownership from its Namespace and resolves the owner at read time by walking at most two hops (CI → Namespace → Party). This keeps ownership correct when it changes — update the namespace, and ten thousand CIs reflect it instantly — but moves the cost to read time, where it must stay fast. The target is ≤50ms at p99 against 100,000 CIs; risk R3 names this latency budget as a gating launch criterion, and the resolver lives in CMP8 alongside the computed-edge logic.

The closing argument

The case against, made honestly.

A document that only argued for OneOps would not be trustworthy enough to argue with. This section makes the competitor's case, concedes what is fair in it, names the one customer profile where v1.0 lands weakest, and states the signals over the next twelve months that would prove the bet right — or wrong.

§ 09 · The honest case against

Four objections, three strong segments, one weak one.

The four objections below are the ones a ServiceNow or UCMDB account team would raise in a competitive deal, stated as they would state them (Task 12 · Pt 4). Each gets the honest response — not a rebuttal that makes it disappear, but the part that is fair conceded and the part that is answerable answered. An objection with no concession in the reply is a sign the reply is dishonest.

01
The case against · "Unproven"
"There are no production deployments at scale. You are buying a thesis."

Fair, and unavoidable for a v1.0. The mitigation is structural rather than rhetorical: the three validation scenarios exist precisely to convert the thesis into measured numbers before launch, and the launch criteria (§8) refuse the release until those numbers hold. But the concession stands — until there are reference customers running this in anger, OneOps is a well-reasoned bet, and the reasoning is not the same as the evidence. This document's job is to make the bet legible, not to claim it is already won.

02
The case against · "Narrow"
"It is built for Kubernetes-native mid-market and is weaker everywhere else."

True — and deliberate, which is the only honest defense. The narrowness is the strategy: a system of record that tried to be equally good for every estate would inherit exactly the empty-framework fate the design rejects. OneOps is sharp for the cloud-native mid-market wedge and admits softness in VMware-heavy and regulated-enterprise estates (the weak profile below). The objection is correct about the fact and wrong only if it assumes breadth is always a virtue; for a challenger, a sharp wedge beats a dull plain.

03
The case against · "'AI-native' is marketing"
"Strip the label and it is a graph database with an LLM query box."

Partly fair, and worth conceding cleanly: the phrase "AI-native" oversells, and OneOps should not lean on it. What is real underneath is narrower and more defensible — an agent surface that is read-only behind an approval gate, natural language as the primary query path instead of a TQL dialect, and, most importantly, the evidence-and-provenance discipline (P03) that lets an AI reason over the graph without fabricating, because every fact carries how it is known. That is a genuine architectural stance. "AI-native" as a slogan is not; the substance is the provenance model, not the label.

04
The case against · "The differentiators are niche"
"Each of the four white-space features matters only to a slice of the market."

True, and already conceded in §4's note on narrowness — a differentiator that mattered equally to everyone would differentiate for no one. Namespace inheritance matters at Kubernetes scale; the promotion workflow matters where dependency curation is a real practice; genealogy matters to audit-heavy buyers. The honest form of the objection is not "these are niche" but "OneOps's growth depends on those niches being large enough and growing," which is exactly the bet — and the segment analysis below is where that bet is either validated or not.

Where v1.0 lands — three strong segments, one weak

Four customer profiles, ranked by how well v1.0 serves them (Task 12 · Pt 3). The ranking is the segmentation strategy made concrete: OneOps is not for everyone yet, and saying so is more useful than a TAM slide that pretends otherwise.

P1
Strongest · the primary wedge
The mid-market Kubernetes adopter

The estate the ontology was designed around — cloud-native, namespace-organized, fast-changing, and underserved by incumbents whose models predate Kubernetes. Every differentiator points here, the discovery depth matches the estate, and the buyer feels the velocity-mismatch pain incumbents cannot fix. This is where OneOps wins first and most cleanly.

P2
Strong · the largest near-term spend
The ServiceNow-frustrated enterprise

The buyer with budget and a concrete grievance — the CMDB that never achieved value, the class explosion, the consulting bill to change anything. The migration verdict is moderate-to-painful (Appendix C), so the deal is real but not frictionless; the wedge is the specific failure they have already lived, met with a model that does not repeat it.

P3
Strong · the low-cost proof
The greenfield team

No incumbent to migrate from, no sunk model to unwind. The prescriptive default (P07) is the whole pitch: a working ontology out of the box instead of a blank canvas. Low deal value individually, but the cheapest place to generate the reference deployments that answer objection AC1 — proof manufactured at low cost.

P4
Weakest · not yet
The regulated enterprise

The honest soft spot. Heavy compliance, deep VMware footprints, security-centric relationship needs that lean on the deferred protectedBy and reaches edges, and a risk tolerance that an unproven v1.0 does not meet. OneOps should not chase this segment at launch — the thin Facility modeling (CR3) and deferred security edges (CR4) land hardest here, and a lost lighthouse deal in a regulated account is expensive. This is a v2 conversation, named as such rather than overpromised.

The twelve-month proof points

The bet is testable, and the signals are known in advance (Task 12 · Pt 5). Naming them now is what makes the next year an experiment rather than a hope.

Signals the bet is proving right

The three validation scenarios hit their thresholds and the graph-store decision resolves cleanly. Mid-market Kubernetes accounts (P1) close on the strength of the four demos. Greenfield deployments (P3) reach value without professional services, validating LC6. Customers describe the dependency graph as legible — the CAP5 floor holds, and the UCMDB cautionary tale is not repeated. Observed-versus-declared (W2) and inherited ownership (W1) are cited by customers as reasons they switched, not features they tolerated.

Signals the bet is proving wrong

The graph store cannot meet the latency and throughput thresholds together, forcing a re-architecture. Namespace-inheritance confusion (CR1) shows up as a recurring support and churn theme rather than an onboarding wrinkle. Discovery depth (CR2) loses deals against incumbents whose scanners simply see more. The differentiators prove too niche — the wedge segments are real but too small to compound into a growth story, turning AC4 from an objection into a diagnosis. If two or more of these hold by month twelve, the bet needs revisiting, not just better execution.

The product is a bet that flat-and-legible beats comprehensive-and-unusable for the customer who has actually tried to use the comprehensive thing. The next twelve months are the wager being settled.
